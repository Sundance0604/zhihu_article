# GEOMETRY, TOPOLOGY AND PHYSICS

> 原文：[知乎专栏](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/2103269066798528196)

## QUANTUM PHYSICS

### Lagrangian Formalism

Define the action

$ S[q]=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}L(q,\dot q,t)\,dt,\qquad L=T-V. $

For a small variation $\delta q$, the first variation of $S$ is, by Taylor expansion,

$ \delta S=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}\left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\delta q+ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\delta\dot q \right)dt. $

Integration by parts gives

$ \int_{t_i}^{t_f}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\delta\dot q\,dt = \left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\delta q\right]_{t_i}^{t_f} -\int_{t_i}^{t_f}\frac{d}{dt} \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\right)\delta q\,dt. $

With fixed endpoints $\delta q(t_i)=\delta q(t_f)=0$,

$ \delta S=\int_{t_i}^{t_f} \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q} -\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\right)\delta q\,dt. $

Since $\delta S=0$ for arbitrary interior variations, the **Euler–Lagrange equation** follows:

$ \boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q} -\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}=0.} $

**Cyclic Coordinates and Conservation**

$q_k$ is a **cyclic coordinate** if $\partial L/\partial q_k=0$. Define its **conjugate momentum** as

$ p_k=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_k}. $

By the Euler–Lagrange equation,

$ \boxed{\frac{dp_k}{dt}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k}=0.} $

Thus, the conjugate momentum of a cyclic coordinate is conserved.

**Noether’s Theorem**

Suppose the action is invariant under a continuous symmetry

$ q_k(t)\rightarrow q_k(t)+\delta q_k(t), \qquad \delta q_k=\varepsilon\xi_k, $

where $\varepsilon$ is a constant infinitesimal parameter.

Without imposing fixed-endpoint variations, integration by parts gives

$ \delta S= \int_{t_i}^{t_f}\sum_k \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k} -\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_k}\right) \delta q_k\,dt + \left[\sum_k p_k\delta q_k\right]_{t_i}^{t_f}. $

Along a physical trajectory, the integral vanishes by the Euler–Lagrange equations. Since the action is invariant under the symmetry, $\delta S=0$. Therefore,

$ \left[\sum_k p_k\xi_k\right]_{t_i}^{t_f}=0. $

As the time interval is arbitrary,

$ \boxed{J=\sum_k p_k\xi_k=\text{constant}.} $

This is **Noether’s theorem** for continuous symmetries that leave the action invariant.

Examples: spatial translations imply momentum conservation; rotations imply angular momentum conservation.

**Gauge Freedom of the Lagrangian**

The Lagrangian is not unique. For any smooth function $Q(q,t)$, define

$ \widetilde L=L+\frac{dQ(q,t)}{dt}. $

Then

$ \widetilde S[q] =S[q]+Q(q(t_f),t_f)-Q(q(t_i),t_i). $

Since the endpoints are fixed in Hamilton’s principle,

$ \delta\widetilde S=\delta S. $

Hence $L$ and $\widetilde L$ yield the same Euler–Lagrange equations. This is the **total-derivative (gauge) freedom** of the Lagrangian.

### **Hamiltonian formalism**

$q$ is Generalized Coordinate. It is different what in Lagrangian. For instance, $L=\frac12m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)-V(x)$ only influenced by force in $x$ , hence in Lagrangian $\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}\neq 0,\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}= 0,$ hence $x$ isn`t cyclic coordinate, $y$ is cyclic coordinate. Both $(x,y)$ are generalized coordinate: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial L}{\partial x}\\[4pt] \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} \end{pmatrix}$ . $p$ is conjugate momentum(here is generalized momentum).

Define $H(q,p)=\sum_k p_k\dot q_k-L(q,\dot q)$ , The space with coordinates $(q_k,p_k)$ is called the phase space. As $dH=\sum_k\left( \dot q_k\,dp_k-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k}dq_k \right)$ , $dH=\sum_k\left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_k}dq_k+ \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k}dp_k \right)$ , so $\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k}=\dot q_k, \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_k}=-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k}$ .

The possion bracket is used to denote $\{A,B\} = \frac{\partial A}{\partial q}\frac{\partial B}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial A}{\partial p}\frac{\partial B}{\partial q}$ . So $\frac{dA}{dt} =\sum_k\left( \frac{\partial A}{\partial q_k}\dot q_k+ \frac{\partial A}{\partial p_k}\dot p_k \right)= \sum_k\left( \frac{\partial A}{\partial q_k}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k} - \frac{\partial A}{\partial p_k}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_k} \right)=\{A,H\}$ In Noether's theorem, $\frac{dQ}{dt}=\{Q,H\}$ , through this can find $\frac{d Q}{dt}=0$ .

### **Heisenberg equation, Heisenberg picture and Schrodinger picture**

Heisenberg equation is:

$\frac{d\hat A}{dt} =\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat A,\hat H]$ the solution is $\hat A(t)= e^{i\hat Ht/\hbar}\hat A(0)e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}$ . Here $U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar} =I-\frac{it}{\hbar}H +\frac{1}{2!}\left(-\frac{it}{\hbar}\right)^2H^2+\cdots$ ，and $U^*U=I$ . So $\langle A\rangle_t =\langle\psi(0)|U^*(t)A(0)U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle$ . This is Schrodinger picture.

Define $|\psi_S(t)\rangle =e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}|\psi_H\rangle$ , so Schrodinger equation is $i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\psi_S(t)\rangle =\hat H|\psi_S(t)\rangle$ .
